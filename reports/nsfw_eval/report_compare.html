Instead, Anderson uses fragmentation: - The scene is told in retrospective flashes, not chronological blow-by-blow action - Sentences break apart—literally shorter, choppier syntax appears - The narrator (Melinda) dissociates: she describes floating outside her body, watching from a distance - Physical details are sensory but non-anatomical: the grass, the party noise fading, a specific smell, the weight of something crushing her - The word "rape" itself isn't used for most of the book—she calls it "IT," refusing to name it, which mirrors real trauma responses ## The Craft Choices That Keep Us With the Victim **1. Absence of eroticized language** No lingering on bodies as objects of beauty or desire. **Content note:** This answer discusses a fictional rape scene. A useful example is **Alice Walker’s *The Color Purple* (1982)**, especially the first letter. Celie, a fourteen-year-old Black girl, writes to God and recounts sexual abuse by the man she calls Pa. The scene is very brief: he tells her she will do what her mother would not; he presses against her, grabs her breasts, penetrates her. When she cries from the pain, he chokes her and tells her to “shut up and git used to it.” She adds that she never gets used to it, and that she now feels sick whenever she has to cook. It is not a dramatized set-piece. It is a compressed, childlike report inside a letter. That brevity is one of the main craft choices that keeps the scene from becoming titillating. Titillation usually needs pacing, sensory lingering, and an erotic frame—slow buildup, attention to bodies, pleasure, suspense. Walker refuses all of that. The act is over in a few sentences. There is no seduction, no romantic atmosphere, no aestheticized description. The reader is not invited to watch; they are made to overhear a victim’s memory. The point of view is also crucial. The novel is epistolary and first-person. We are inside Celie’s consciousness, not Pa’s. We only know what she knows and feels: pain, confusion, fear, nausea, shame. We get no access to the aggressor’s arousal, his thoughts, or his pleasure. He is not a desiring subject with an interior life; he is a threatening force. That alone prevents the reader from being positioned in the aggressor’s gaze. Walker’s language matters too. Celie’s voice is ungrammatical, childlike, and euphemistic. She says “his thing” rather than using adult erotic vocabulary. That limits the scene’s erotic charge. The words are blunt but not pornographic; they register violation, not arousal. The focus stays on coercion and pain: “When that hurt, I cry. He start to choke me.” The only command is silence and endurance: “You better shut up and git used to it.” The reader’s emotional response is horror and empathy, not excitement. The novel also frames the act through the letter to God: Celie is confessing, pleading, surviving. The reader becomes a witness or confidant, not a voyeur.
